III.

How it works.

Four unhurried steps. The worms do the labor; the household keeps the rhythm.

Step one

Feed the trays.

Scraps go in the top — peelings, cores, coffee grounds, eggshells. Keep glossy or waxy coatings out; they carry a plastic film.

Step two

The worms work.

Red wigglers turn the scraps to castings over weeks. No turning, no smell. They keep two weeks without food or water if you travel.

Step three

Draw the tea.

Keep the tap open. Compost tea drips into the basin on its own — dilute to 50–90% water before it reaches the garden.

Step four

Harvest the soil.

After eight weeks, lift the bottom tray and spread the castings — about 6 inches deep around the roots that need it most.

VI.

Questions.

Does it smell?

A balanced digester smells faintly of soil — never of waste. Odor is a sign of overfeeding or too much moisture, both quickly corrected by easing off the scraps for a few days.

What can the worms eat?

Fruit and vegetable scraps, coffee grounds, crushed eggshells, and torn cardboard. Keep out meat, dairy, oils, and anything with a glossy or waxy plastic coating.

How is this different from a countertop food recycler?

A countertop recycler dries and grinds scraps into a dehydrated meal — a dry additive that still has to break down in the soil. The digester runs them through a living colony of worms, returning true vermicompost and compost tea, ready for the bed.

What if I travel?

The colony keeps two weeks without food or water. For longer absences, a deeper feeding and a damp sheet of cardboard will carry it through a month.

How much can I add at once?

Up to 5 gallons in a single day won't hurt the system — a holiday or a big cleanup is no trouble. The steady average is roughly 2 lbs a day.

What is included?
